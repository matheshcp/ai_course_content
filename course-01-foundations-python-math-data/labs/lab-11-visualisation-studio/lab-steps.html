Charts That Communicate Track: Tips + Tickets (4 Publication-Quality Plots)

Scenario: Two briefs, two datasets: (1) tips.csv — who tips more? (2) tickets.csv — SLA status mix; the tickets file contains emails (PII) so redact before any plot leaves your machine. Produce four charts with annotated insights. You will learn: figure anatomy, chart choice, PII redaction, subplots, annotation. Time: ~45 minutes. Level: Beginner. Needs: pandas + matplotlib. Env: 🟢 Colab only.

A chart is an argument with a picture attached. Each of the four plots below answers one question from the two briefs, computes the supporting number first, then draws the figure and annotates that number onto it. Follow that order — number, then chart — and you avoid the classic failure mode of a beautiful plot that doesn’t say anything checkable.

Chart-choice mental map

Pick the chart from the question, not from habit. Distribution questions get histograms or boxes; part-of-whole with few categories gets stacked bars; relationship between two measures gets a scatter; ordered time gets a line. The “Do not use” column is where most bad slides come from.

QuestionChartDo not use
Distribution of a measurehistogram / boxpie for many bins
Share of categoriesstacked/bar (few cats)3-D pie
Relationship bill vs tipscatterbar
Trend over timelinescatter if ordered
Pitfall: PII in charts — emails, names, ticket text — must be redacted before anything is plotted or exported. A PNG saved to disk, pasted into a deck, or uploaded to Colab can leak just as effectively as the raw CSV.

1. Load both datasets (local first, Colab fallback)

Why both files load through one ensure helper: it prefers a local copy and only downloads when missing, so re-runs are fast and partially offline. Reading shapes and headline rates up front ((244, 7) and (107, 9)) gives you the denominators every later percentage will lean on — you can’t caption “mean tip 16.1%” credibly if you don’t know it’s over 244 bills.

import os, re
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def ensure(name, url):
    if not os.path.exists(name):
        import urllib.request
        urllib.request.urlretrieve(url, name)
    return name

ensure("tips.csv",
       "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mwaskom/seaborn-data/master/tips.csv")
ensure("tickets.csv",
       "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vihar/datasets/master/tickets.csv")

tips = pd.read_csv("tips.csv")
tickets = pd.read_csv("tickets.csv")
print(tips.shape, tickets.shape)  # (244, 7) (107, 9)
tips["tip_pct"] = tips["tip"] / tips["total_bill"]
print(tips["tip_pct"].mean().round(4))  # 0.1608 overall
print(tickets["status"].value_counts())
# open 42, in-progress 34, closed 31

What to notice: tips is 244 rows × 7 columns, tickets is 107 rows × 9 columns; the derived tip_pct averages 0.1608 (≈16.1%) across all bills; ticket statuses split open 42 · in-progress 34 · closed 31, which sums to 107 — every status accounted for, no null bucket. matplotlib.use("Agg") renders figures to files without a GUI, which is what keeps these cells portable in Colab.

2. Redact PII before plotting tickets

Why redaction is its own section: email addresses live in ordinary string columns and survive careless copies — into value_counts, axis labels, hover text, or an exported PNG. A regex sweep with an explicit zero-hits post-condition converts “I think I cleaned it” into a checked invariant, and doing it on a copy() leaves the raw frame intact for any authorised audit.

EMAIL_RE = re.compile(r"[A-Za-z0-9._%+-]+@[A-Za-z0-9.-]+\.[A-Za-z]{2,}")

def redact_email(s):
    return EMAIL_RE.sub("[EMAIL]", str(s))

# How much PII is in here?
raw_hits = sum(len(EMAIL_RE.findall(str(v))) for v in tickets.astype(str).values.ravel())
print("email-like hits before redaction:", raw_hits)  # 184+

tickets_clean = tickets.copy()
for col in tickets_clean.columns:
    tickets_clean[col] = tickets_clean[col].apply(
        lambda v: redact_email(v) if isinstance(v, str) and "@" in str(v) else v
    )

still = sum(len(EMAIL_RE.findall(str(v))) for v in tickets_clean.astype(str).values.ravel())
print("hits after redaction:", still)  # 0
assert still == 0

What to notice: the sweep finds 184+ email-like hits in the raw frame and 0 after redaction, and the assert enforces that zero. Only string cells containing @ are rewritten, so numeric columns (and their dtypes) pass through untouched — chart 3’s counts remain exactly the pre-redaction counts.

Redaction is pre-plot hygiene. Don’t put raw emails in chart titles, legends, or exported PNGs.
Pitfall: redact PII before the first plot() call, not after saving. Matplotlib embeds whatever strings you feed it into titles, tick labels, and legends; a “quick look” PNG is already an artefact that can leave your machine.

3. Chart 1 — Tip % distribution by day (histogram overlay)

Why a distribution first: before claiming any group difference, you need to see the spread. Overlaid histograms with alpha=0.45 show whether Friday’s higher mean comes from a shifted peak or a few outlier tippers, and the per-day n= in each legend label keeps sample size visible — Sat/Sun carry far more bills than Fri.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
for day, sub in tips.groupby("day"):
    ax.hist(sub["tip_pct"], bins=15, alpha=0.45, label=f"{day} (n={len(sub)})")
ax.set_xlabel("Tip % of bill")
ax.set_ylabel("Count")
ax.set_title("Tip percentage distribution by day")
ax.legend()
ax.annotate("Overall mean ≈ 16.1%",
            xy=(0.1608, tips.groupby("day").size().max() * 0.45),
            fontsize=9)
fig.tight_layout(); fig.savefig("chart1_tip_pct_by_day.png", dpi=120)
print("saved chart1_tip_pct_by_day.png")

What to notice: the annotation anchors the overall mean ≈ 16.1% (0.1608) as a landmark against every day’s bars; most mass sits in a band around that mean rather than in long tails, which is why the mean comparisons in Exercise 1 are fair starting points.

Mean tip % by day (for the caption): Fri 17.0% · Thur 16.1% · Sun 16.7% · Sat 15.3%.

4. Chart 2 — Mean tip % by day + time (grouped bar)

Why group by sitting: “who tips more” often hides a when effect — the same day can behave differently at lunch and dinner. The pivot table computes the day×sitting cell means explicitly (printed first, so numbers are checkable), then the grouped bar renders them; fixed reindex(["Thur", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]) keeps weekday order stable across saves.

pivot = tips.pivot_table(index="day", columns="time",
                         values="tip_pct", aggfunc="mean").reindex(["Thur", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"])
print(pivot.round(4))

ax = pivot.plot(kind="bar", figsize=(7, 4), rot=0)
ax.set_ylabel("Mean tip %")
ax.set_title("Mean tip % by day and sitting")
ax.set_ylim(0, 0.25)
ax.legend(title="Sitting")
fig = ax.get_figure(); fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("chart2_tip_pct_day_time.png", dpi=120)
print("saved chart2_tip_pct_day_time.png")

What to notice: ylim=(0, 0.25) starts the axis at zero — bars only compare honestly from a true baseline — and the printed pivot shows every day/sitting cell before it’s drawn. The overall sitting comparison (Lunch 16.4% vs Dinner 16.0%) is only ~0.4 pp: a small gap worth stating with its magnitude rather than as a headline.

Insight to annotate: lunch tips slightly higher on average (Lunch 16.4% vs Dinner 16.0% overall).

5. Chart 3 — Tickets: stacked status × priority (redacted data only)

Why stacked bars here: the brief is part-of-whole across a small set of categories (three priorities, three statuses) — exactly where stacked bars beat pies. Building the crosstab on tickets_clean (not tickets) is the point of the exercise: counts and colour encode status mix, and no email ever reaches the figure. Reordering columns to open → in-progress → closed makes the stack read as a pipeline.

ct = pd.crosstab(tickets_clean["priority"], tickets_clean["status"])
# order columns for readability
for c in ["open", "in-progress", "closed"]:
    if c not in ct.columns:
        ct[c] = 0
ct = ct[["open", "in-progress", "closed"]]
print(ct)

ax = ct.plot(kind="bar", stacked=True, figsize=(7, 4), rot=0,
             color=["#4C72B0", "#DD8452", "#55A868"])
ax.set_ylabel("Tickets")
ax.set_title("Ticket status mix by priority (PII redacted)")
ax.annotate("Open work concentrated in low/med priority",
            xy=(0.02, 0.92), xycoords="axes fraction", fontsize=9)
fig = ax.get_figure(); fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("chart3_tickets_status.png", dpi=120)
print("saved chart3_tickets_status.png")

What to notice: the title itself declares “(PII redacted)”, so anyone who sees the PNG knows the provenance; column totals still equal the Section 1 counts — open 42 · in-progress 34 · closed 31 (n = 107) — because redaction changed strings, not rows. The annotation names the pattern (“open work concentrated in low/med priority”) instead of leaving the reader to squint at segment heights.

Expected status totals: open 42 · in-progress 34 · closed 31 (n = 107).

6. Chart 4 — Total bill vs tip scatter (annotated insight)

Why scatter for the relationship brief: bill and tip are two continuous measures, so each table is a point; colouring by sex with the mean tip % inside the legend label puts the summary stat next to the evidence. The arrow annotation on the max bill turns an outlier into a labelled observation instead of an unexplained dot in the corner.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
colors = {"Male": "#4C72B0", "Female": "#C44E52"}
for sex, sub in tips.groupby("sex"):
    ax.scatter(sub["total_bill"], sub["tip"], s=28, alpha=0.7,
               label=f"{sex} (mean tip% {sub['tip_pct'].mean():.1%})",
               color=colors.get(sex, None))
ax.set_xlabel("Total bill ($)")
ax.set_ylabel("Tip ($)")
ax.set_title("Tip vs total bill")
ax.legend()
# annotate the largest bill
i = tips["total_bill"].idxmax()
ax.annotate(f"max bill ${tips.loc[i, 'total_bill']:.2f}",
            xy=(tips.loc[i, "total_bill"], tips.loc[i, "tip"]),
            xytext=(tips.loc[i, "total_bill"] - 12, tips.loc[i, "tip"] + 1.2),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", lw=0.8))
fig.tight_layout(); fig.savefig("chart4_tip_vs_bill.png", dpi=120)
print("saved chart4_tip_vs_bill.png")

What to notice: points rise together — larger bills get larger tips in dollars — but tip percentage still clusters in the 15–20% band even at the $50.81 maximum, which is why percentage (not dollars) is the fair cross-group comparison. The legend already carries each group’s mean: Female ≈ 16.7%, Male ≈ 15.8%.

Female mean tip % ≈ 16.7% · Male ≈ 15.8% (small gap, don’t over-claim).

Pitfall: a ~0.9 pp gap between group means on 244 observational rows is a weak signal, not a finding. Report it with the numbers, avoid causal language, and remember day/time/party-size confounders weren’t controlled here — spurious correlation is one groupby away.
